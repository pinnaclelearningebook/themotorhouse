Selling a car with finance outstanding is normal. It is not a problem to be confessed, it does not weaken your position, and any buyer who treats it as a complication is telling you something about them rather than about your car.

What it does require is understanding the mechanics, because there is one fact underneath all of it that changes what you are allowed to do.

## The fact everything else follows from

On hire purchase and on a personal contract purchase, **you do not own the car**. The finance company does, until the agreement is settled.

That is not a technicality. It means you cannot sell the car, transfer clean title to a buyer, scrap it or export it while the agreement is live. It also means any legitimate buyer will run a provenance check before parting with money, the outstanding finance will show up on it, and a sale that ignores it will not complete.

So the whole process is really one question: how does the finance get settled, and by whom.

## First, work out which agreement you actually have

People use "on finance" to mean four quite different things, and they do not behave the same way.

**Hire purchase (HP).** You are buying the car in instalments. The lender owns it until the final payment. You can settle early and sell.

**Personal contract purchase (PCP).** Same ownership position, different structure — lower monthly payments with a large optional final payment at the end. You can settle early and sell, and the settlement figure will account for that final payment.

**Personal contract hire (PCH), or a lease.** You are renting. You never own the car and there is no route to owning it. **You cannot sell a leased car**, and no legitimate buyer will offer to buy one from you. If this is your agreement, the conversation you need is with your leasing company about early termination, not with us or anyone like us.

**A personal loan.** The money is unsecured and in your name. The car is yours outright, you can sell it whenever you like, and the loan is simply a debt you continue to owe. This is the easiest case and many people do not realise it is the one they are in.

If you are not certain which you have, the agreement will say, and your lender will tell you.

## The settlement figure

For HP and PCP, the settlement figure is the number that clears the agreement and releases the car.

You have a statutory right to settle early and to be told what that costs. It comes from the [Consumer Credit Act 1974](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/1974/39/part/VII/crossheading/early-payment-by-debtor), and Citizens Advice sets out [how to ask for one](https://www.citizensadvice.org.uk/debt-and-money/borrowing-money/paying-off-a-credit-agreement-early/). You write to or call the lender and ask for an early settlement figure. They have to provide it.

What it contains: the outstanding capital, interest accrued to the settlement date, any instalments you have missed, and potentially an early settlement charge. On a PCP it accounts for the optional final payment, which is why PCP settlement figures often surprise people who have been looking only at their monthly payments.

What it does not contain: a penalty for daring to sell. Early settlement charges are capped. [MoneyHelper sets out the limits](https://www.moneyhelper.org.uk/en/everyday-money/cut-your-car-finance-hire-purchase-and-other-finance-costs) — broadly, no more than <Data>1%</Data> of the amount repaid early, or <Data>0.5%</Data> where less than twelve months remain, and no additional charge at all where the early repayment is under <Data>£8,000</Data>. If a lender quotes something that looks unlike that, ask them to break it down.

**Settlement figures expire.** They are calculated to a specific date, because interest accrues daily. The figure you are given will state the date it is valid until, commonly a week or two out. Check that date, and if your sale is going to take longer, ask for a fresh one rather than assuming the old number still holds.

## Then compare two numbers

Once you have a settlement figure and an offer for the car, the entire situation reduces to a comparison.

**If the offer is higher than the settlement figure**, you are in equity. The buyer pays the lender what it is owed, you receive the difference, and the agreement closes. Nothing further to think about.

**If the offer is lower than the settlement figure**, you are in negative equity. The car is worth less than you owe on it. This is common, particularly early in a PCP, and it is not a disaster. It means you pay the shortfall — you make up the difference so the agreement can be cleared and the car released.

Negative equity is where sellers get taken advantage of, because it is the moment you feel you have no options. You have more than you think.

## The option nobody at a dealership mentions

If you are in negative equity and you have had the agreement a while, look at **voluntary termination** before you sell.

Under the Consumer Credit Act, once you have paid half the total amount payable under the agreement, you have the right to end it and hand the car back. Citizens Advice and MoneyHelper both describe how it works. If you have not yet reached the halfway point you can make up the difference to get there.

The important caveats, stated properly rather than buried:

- You get nothing back. If you have paid more than half, that money does not come back to you.
- The car has to be in reasonable condition. You can be charged for damage beyond fair wear and tear.
- It appears on your credit file. It is a legitimate right rather than a default, but it is visible and lenders can see it.

Voluntary termination is not right for most people. But if you owe considerably more than the car is worth and you have passed the halfway mark, it can be a better outcome than paying a shortfall to sell — and it is the one route no buyer will ever volunteer to you, including us, unless you ask. So we are telling you about it up front.

## How the sale itself works

In practice, with a buyer who does this regularly:

1. You get a settlement figure from your lender.
2. You get an offer for the car and check it against that figure.
3. The buyer pays the settlement amount directly to the finance company, not to you. Nobody should be asking you to take the whole sum and settle it yourself.
4. Any balance is paid to you.
5. The finance company confirms the agreement is closed.

That last step matters and gets skipped. **You remain liable until the lender confirms in writing that the balance is cleared.** Get the confirmation, and keep it.

## What to watch for

A buyer who wants to pay you the full amount and leave you to settle the finance is creating a problem for you rather than solving one. The money should go to the lender.

A buyer who reduces their offer once they learn about the finance is not pricing risk, they are testing you. The presence of finance does not change what the car is worth.
