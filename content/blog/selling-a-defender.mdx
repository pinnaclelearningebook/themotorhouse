If you own a Defender you are in a different position from almost every other seller we speak to, and it is worth understanding why before you accept anybody's number.

## The outlier in the range

Most of the Land Rover range depreciates the way premium SUVs generally do. The Defender has not behaved like that. Since launch it has held its value unusually well, supply has been managed tightly, and demand has stayed ahead of it.

We are not going to attach a percentage to that, because we have not seen a figure we would stand behind and inventing one would be worse than saying so. What we will say is that the practical experience of selling a Defender is different from selling almost anything else with the same badge on it: you should expect a stronger number, and you should be more sceptical of a weak one.

That scepticism is the useful part. On a car that depreciates conventionally, a disappointing offer might simply reflect the market. On a Defender, a disappointing offer is more likely to reflect the buyer.

## The misinformation that costs Defender owners money

This is the single most common way we see Defender owners talked down, and it rests on a confusion between two engines.

**If someone has told you the D300 has a timing chain problem, they have mixed it up with a different engine.**

The widely discussed Ingenium timing chain issue belongs to the **2.0-litre four-cylinder** diesel. On that engine, chain wear is a real and documented concern, driven substantially by oil dilution when diesel particulate filter regeneration cycles start and fail to complete — which is why short-journey cars are more exposed than motorway ones.

The D300 is not that engine. It is the **3.0-litre straight-six**, a different unit with a different reputation: a revised chain arrangement, better heat management, and largely free of the oil dilution problem that drives wear on the smaller engine. It is generally regarded as one of the sounder engines in the family.

The pattern is predictable. A buyer references a well-known problem. The owner has no particular reason to doubt it, because the problem genuinely exists — just not on their car. The offer drops, and it drops on a misunderstanding.

If that happens to you, ask which engine they mean. The question usually ends the conversation.

## What actually moves a Defender's number

**Body style.** A 90, a 110 and a 130 are different cars commercially, with different buyers and different demand. This is the first thing to establish and it is not a detail.

**Option packs.** The Defender was sold with packs that were expensive when new, and the used market still recognises them. As with any heavily optioned car, most owners underdescribe their own vehicle. Find the original order before you get valuations.

**Whether it is a Hard Top commercial.** Commercial versions sit in a different tax position from passenger cars. If yours is one, say so and mention whether you are VAT registered, because it changes how the offer should be structured rather than just what it is.

**Modifications.** These cut both ways. A tastefully modified Defender appeals to a narrower group than a standard one, and standard cars generally travel better. If you have modified yours, the key question is whether you still have the original parts — having the takeoffs can matter more than owners expect.

**Genuine off-road use.** Not disqualifying, and not something to hide. Tell us and we will ask a couple of sensible questions.

## Why our number on a Defender is often stronger

Two things are true at once here, and the combination is unusual.

The Defender holds its value well in the UK, so the domestic floor under it is high.

It is also one of the models our Cyprus buyers ask for by name. Cyprus drives on the left, right-hand-drive cars are the local standard, and a young, well-specified Defender is close to an ideal car for that market. We set out the full mechanism, and its limits, on [the export page](/export) and in [why UK cars get exported](/blog/why-uk-cars-are-exported).

Strong home-market values and strong export demand at the same time is a rare combination. It is the reason Defender offers tend to surprise people, and it is why the five-year boundary matters more on this car than most — under it, both forces are working for you.

If your Defender is older than that, we still buy it, and it is still a car with an unusually solid domestic market underneath it. The offer is simply priced against the UK trade rather than an export buyer, and we would rather say so than imply a premium that is not there.

## Why demand for this one is genuinely different

It is worth separating two explanations that get blurred together, because only one of them is durable.

The weaker explanation is novelty. A new Defender arrives, there is a queue, prices are firm for a while, and eventually the queue clears and it behaves like everything else. If that were the whole story, the sensible advice would be to sell quickly before the effect wears off.

The stronger explanation, and the one we think actually holds, is that the Defender occupies a position almost nothing else does. It is a genuinely capable vehicle that is also comfortable enough to use every day, wearing a badge with real history behind it, in a market where most rivals have drifted towards being road cars with raised suspension. Buyers who want that specific combination have very few alternatives, and a buyer with few alternatives is a buyer who pays.

That second thing does not evaporate when the waiting lists clear, which is why we treat the Defender as structurally different rather than temporarily fashionable.

There is a third factor that applies to us specifically rather than to the market generally. The characteristics that make a Defender desirable here — capability, specification, the right-hand-drive configuration, condition — are the same characteristics our Cyprus buyers are looking for. On most models, domestic demand and export demand pull on different things. On this one they pull on the same things, which is why the two effects compound rather than cancelling.

## Should you wait?

Some Defender owners ask this, and it deserves a straight answer rather than a sales one.

Waiting is a legitimate choice. These have held value well and there is no countdown running.

The honest counterweight is that a Defender still depreciates, more slowly than most but not at zero, and it costs you tax and insurance every month it sits on the drive after you have decided to sell it. If you are waiting for a reason, wait. If you are waiting because selling feels like effort, that is a different thing and it is costing you something real.

## Before you accept any offer

Establish the body style and the exact trim.

List the option packs properly.

Ask which engine the buyer thinks you have, if you have a D300 and their number seems low.

And get more than one offer. On a car this strong, the spread between a buyer who knows what a Defender is worth and one working from a generic valuation is wider than on almost anything else we handle. We would rather compete against the other numbers than against your uncertainty.
